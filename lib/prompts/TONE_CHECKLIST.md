# Neural Space Tone & Structure Checklist

Use this before submitting an article to verify it matches Neural Space's voice.

---

## SIMPLIFIED VERSION

### Opening (First 3 lines MUST do ONE of these)

- [ ] **Hook 1 - Surprising Fact**: "Every time you sleep, your brain is deliberately erasing memories."
- [ ] **Hook 2 - Pressing Question**: "What if the thing keeping you alive is also slowly breaking your DNA?"
- [ ] **Hook 3 - Clear Stakes**: "The bacteria in your gut might be more responsible for your mood than your therapist."

**✓ PASS**: Reader is immediately curious and reads line 2
**✗ FAIL**: Reader skims or closes the tab

---

### Structure

**Opening (sentences 1-3)**
- [ ] Hook ✓
- [ ] Why this matters NOW (not 20 years from now, but today)
- [ ] Who discovered this? What lab? Why were they researching this?

**Middle (body paragraphs)**
- [ ] Start with simplest explanation
- [ ] Gradually layer in complexity
- [ ] Use analogies (explain BEFORE using)
- [ ] Include a human detail (frustration, breakthrough, "eureka!" moment)
- [ ] Real-world implications woven in
- [ ] Keep paragraphs SHORT when explaining complex ideas (2-3 sentences)

**Ending**
- [ ] Flip back to the reader: "What does this mean for you?"
- [ ] End with a lingering question or thought
- [ ] NOT "In conclusion, this is very important"

---

## SCIENTIFIC VERSION

### Opening

- [ ] Clear research question stated
- [ ] Methodological approach summarized
- [ ] Key findings preview WITH citations (Author et al., Year)

**Example**: "Recent work (Smith et al., 2023) demonstrated that X affects Y through mechanism Z..."

---

### Structure

**Each claim must have**
- [ ] Integrated citation: (Author Year) or Author et al. (Year)
- [ ] Effect size or data if available
- [ ] Explanation of mechanism/how it works
- [ ] Limitations or caveats acknowledged

**Organization**
- [ ] Logical flow (build on previous claims)
- [ ] Subheadings guide technical readers
- [ ] Terminology used correctly
- [ ] No unexplained jargon on first use

**Bibliography**
- [ ] Full author list (or "et al.")
- [ ] Year
- [ ] Full paper title
- [ ] Journal name
- [ ] DOI (if available)

**Ending**
- [ ] Limitations acknowledged (what we still don't know)
- [ ] Implications for the field
- [ ] Open questions for future research
- [ ] Where does this fit in the broader landscape?

---

## TONE COMPARISON

### ❌ WRONG TONE
- Neutral, sterile, purely informative
- "Recent research has shown..."
- Generic opening without hook
- No personality
- Citations as footnotes only
- Buries the lede

### ✓ RIGHT TONE
- Curious, engaging, slightly narrative
- "Scientists just discovered..."
- Strong opening that makes you want to read
- Personality (but never clickbait)
- Citations woven into narrative
- Lede is the hook

---

## RED FLAGS (If you see these, rewrite)

- [ ] **Boring intro**: No hook, no "why should I care"
- [ ] **Missing "why now"**: Doesn't explain current relevance
- [ ] **No human element**: Feels like a Wikipedia entry
- [ ] **All complex at once**: Doesn't build complexity gradually
- [ ] **Citation-free simplified**: Should still cite, just integrate smoothly
- [ ] **Jargon salad in scientific**: Technical terms used without explanation first time
- [ ] **Speculative without caveat**: "This could mean..." without saying it's speculative
- [ ] **Wrong ending**: Summarizes instead of implying

---

## TESTING (Before Publishing)

Read the simplified version out loud:
- [ ] Does it sound like a curious friend telling you something interesting?
- [ ] Would you keep reading past paragraph 2?
- [ ] Could you explain it to someone who knows nothing about the topic?

Read the scientific version:
- [ ] Can you trace every claim back to a citation?
- [ ] Do you understand the limitations?
- [ ] Do you know what future questions this opens up?

## WORD COUNT TARGETS

- **Simplified**: 1,500–2,500 words (5–8 min read)
- **Scientific**: 1,800–3,000 words (7–10 min read)
- **Summary**: 1–2 sentences with stakes + human element

## EXAMPLES OF STRONG STRUCTURES

### Pattern 1: The Surprising Discovery
1. Hook: "Scientists just discovered something nobody expected..."
2. Why now: "This changes our understanding of..."
3. How they found it: "A team at [Lab] was trying to..."
4. What it means: "If this holds up, we might be able to..."

### Pattern 2: The Problem-Solution
1. Hook: "We've known for decades that [problem] exists..."
2. Why it matters: "This affects [millions/our future/our understanding]..."
3. The new solution: "Now researchers have found a way to..."
4. What changes: "This could finally let us..."

### Pattern 3: The Counterintuitive Finding
1. Hook: "Everything we thought was true about X is wrong."
2. What we believed: "For years, scientists believed..."
3. The plot twist: "But a new study shows..."
4. What it means: "If this is right, we need to rethink..."
